SECTION 1: WHAT IS THIS PROJECT?
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

This project is a fully functional Leveraged Buyout (LBO) financial model built
entirely in Python. It replicates the core analytical workflow that a Private Equity
analyst performs when evaluating whether to acquire a company.

The model takes a target company (Devyani International — KFC/Pizza Hut India
franchisee), simulates acquiring it using 65% debt and 35% equity, projects 5 years
of financial performance, models how the company's own cash flows repay the
acquisition debt, and finally calculates the return on investment when the company
is sold.

The output answers the single most important question in PE:

    "If we buy this company today, how much money do we make when we sell it?"

This is measured by two numbers:
    — IRR  (Internal Rate of Return)     : annualized % return on equity invested
    — MoM  (Money-on-Money Multiple)     : how many times you got your money back

The project also stress-tests the deal across multiple scenarios and deal structures,
producing the kind of investment analysis that gets presented to a PE investment
committee before committing hundreds of crores to a deal.

SECTION 2: THE BUSINESS CONCEPT — WHAT IS AN LBO?
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

A Leveraged Buyout is the acquisition of a company using a combination of equity
(your own money) and a large amount of debt (borrowed money). The acquired company's
own cash flows are then used to repay the debt over the holding period.

ANALOGY:
   Buying a house worth ₹1 Cr. You pay ₹35 Lakh of your own money and take
   a ₹65 Lakh mortgage. The house itself earns rental income that pays the
   mortgage. After 5 years, you sell the house — now worth ₹1.5 Cr — and
   pocket the difference after repaying remaining mortgage. That profit on
   your ₹35 Lakh original investment is your "return."

   An LBO works the same way, but instead of a house, you're buying a company.
   Instead of rent, the company's free cash flow repays the debt.

WHY USE DEBT?
   Debt amplifies equity returns. Here's the math:

   Without leverage:
     Buy company for ₹960 Cr with 100% equity.
     Sell after 5 years for ₹1,400 Cr.
     Return = 1400/960 = 1.46x (7.8% IRR)

   With leverage (65% debt):
     Buy company for ₹960 Cr with ₹336 Cr equity + ₹624 Cr debt.
     Sell after 5 years for ₹1,400 Cr. Remaining debt = ₹251 Cr.
     Equity proceeds = 1400 - 251 = ₹1,149 Cr
     Return = 1149/336 = 3.4x (27.8% IRR)

   Same company. Same growth. But the return nearly DOUBLES because of leverage.
   This is the fundamental logic of private equity investing.

THE THREE DRIVERS OF LBO RETURNS:
   Every rupee of profit in an LBO comes from exactly three sources:

   1. EBITDA GROWTH
      The company becomes more profitable over the holding period.
      Higher EBITDA at exit → higher enterprise value at exit.
      This is the "operational value creation" component.

   2. DEBT PAYDOWN
      Every rupee of debt repaid = one more rupee of equity at exit.
      The company literally pays for itself over time.
      In a well-structured LBO, 50-70% of debt is repaid by exit.

   3. MULTIPLE EXPANSION
      If you buy at 8x EBITDA and sell at 9x EBITDA, that 1-turn difference
      applied to a larger exit EBITDA creates significant additional value.
      Example: Exit EBITDA = ₹215 Cr. One extra turn = ₹215 Cr more EV.

   The "return attribution" module in this project decomposes IRR into
   these three buckets — this is what separates senior PE analysts from juniors.

WHAT MAKES A GOOD LBO TARGET?
   Not every company can be acquired via LBO. The ideal target has:
   — Stable, predictable free cash flow (to service debt)
   — Strong EBITDA margins (more cash generation)
   — Low existing debt (room to add acquisition debt)
   — Asset-light model (less capex needed)
   — Defensible market position (revenue won't collapse)
   — Clear exit path (another PE firm or IPO can buy it)

   Devyani International fits most of these criteria — it's a QSR franchise
   with recurring customer demand, manageable capex, and a trackable margin profile.

FILE 1: assumptions.py
────────────────────────────────────────

PURPOSE:
   The single source of truth for every number in the model. If you change
   one number here, the entire downstream model updates automatically.
   In professional PE shops, this is called the "input sheet."

KEY CONTENTS:
   — Company name and base year
   — Entry EV/EBITDA multiple (what you pay)
   — Entry EBITDA (company's earnings at time of purchase)
   — Exit EV/EBITDA multiple (what you sell at)
   — Holding period (how many years you own it)
   — Debt percentage (what fraction of EV is funded by debt)
   — Interest rate on debt
   — Revenue base and CAGR (growth rate)
   — EBITDA margin (profitability as % of revenue)
   — Depreciation, Capex, Working Capital percentages
   — Tax rate
   — Cash sweep flag (True = use all FCF to repay debt aggressively)

COMPUTED PROPERTIES (auto-calculated from inputs):
   — entry_ev        = entry_year_ebitda × entry_ev_multiple
   — total_debt      = entry_ev × debt_pct
   — equity_invested = entry_ev × (1 - debt_pct)

DESIGN CHOICE:
   Uses Python's @dataclass decorator for clean, type-annotated structure.
   @property decorators compute derived values automatically — no need to
   manually update entry_ev whenever you change entry_multiple.

EXAMPLE NUMBERS (base case):
   Entry EBITDA      = ₹120 Cr
   Entry Multiple    = 8x
   Entry EV          = ₹960 Cr
   Debt (65%)        = ₹624 Cr
   Equity (35%)      = ₹336 Cr
   Interest Rate     = 10%
   Revenue Base      = ₹800 Cr
   Revenue CAGR      = 12%
   EBITDA Margin     = 18%
   Exit Multiple     = 9x
   Holding Period    = 5 years

HOW TO USE:
   from assumptions import DealAssumptions
   deal = DealAssumptions()           # loads defaults
   deal.entry_ev_multiple = 7.5      # override any value
   deal.summary()                     # prints deal snapshot

────────────────────────────────────────
FILE 2: income_statement.py
────────────────────────────────────────

PURPOSE:
   Projects a 5-year Income Statement and computes Free Cash Flow (FCF)
   for each year. FCF is the critical output — it's what funds debt repayment.

THE INCOME STATEMENT WATERFALL:
   Revenue                                     (grows at CAGR each year)
   × EBITDA Margin
   = EBITDA                                    (operating cash generation)
   − Depreciation & Amortization (D&A)         (non-cash charge)
   = EBIT                                      (operating profit)
   − Interest Expense                          (cost of LBO debt)
   = EBT (Earnings Before Tax)
   − Tax (25% of positive EBT only)
   = Net Income

   FREE CASH FLOW CALCULATION:
   EBITDA                                      (start from EBITDA, not net income)
   − Tax                                       (actual cash tax paid)
   − Capex                                     (cash spent on assets)
   − Change in Working Capital                 (cash absorbed by operations)
   = FREE CASH FLOW                            (real cash available for debt repayment)

   WHY START FCF FROM EBITDA (NOT NET INCOME)?
   Because D&A is a non-cash expense. It reduces net income on paper but
   no actual cash leaves the company. So we add it back. Starting from
   EBITDA and subtracting only real cash costs gives true cash generation.

KEY FUNCTION: project_income_statement(assumptions, debt_schedule)
   — Takes DealAssumptions object
   — Optionally takes debt_schedule (list of opening debt balances per year)
     to compute accurate interest expense. Without it, uses simplified flat debt.
   — Returns a pandas DataFrame: rows = line items, columns = projection years

IMPORTANT NOTE ON INTEREST EXPENSE:
   Interest expense depends on the opening debt balance each year.
   But the opening debt balance depends on prior year FCF.
   And FCF depends on interest expense (via tax impact).
   This creates a CIRCULAR DEPENDENCY — resolved in debt_schedule.py.

────────────────────────────────────────
FILE 3: debt_schedule.py
────────────────────────────────────────

PURPOSE:
   Models how acquisition debt gets paid down year by year using the
   company's Free Cash Flow. Also tracks the leverage ratio each year.
   This is the mechanical heart of the LBO model.

THE CIRCULAR DEPENDENCY PROBLEM:
   Income Statement needs interest expense to compute FCF.
   Interest expense = opening debt × interest rate.
   Opening debt depends on prior year's closing debt.
   Closing debt = opening debt − repayment.
   Repayment depends on FCF.
   FCF depends on interest expense.
   CIRCLE: FCF → repayment → debt balance → interest → FCF

HOW WE SOLVE IT (Iterative Convergence):
   Step 1: Start with simplified assumption — debt stays flat all 5 years.
           Compute IS and FCF using this simplified interest expense.
   Step 2: Use that FCF to build a first debt schedule.
           Now we have real opening balances for each year.
   Step 3: Feed those real opening balances back into the IS.
           Recompute FCF with accurate interest expense.
   Step 4: Rebuild debt schedule with new FCF.
   Step 5: Repeat until closing balances stop changing (< ₹0.01 Cr difference).
           Usually converges in 3-5 iterations.

   This is exactly what Excel does when you enable "circular references"
   with iterative calculation. We're replicating that logic in Python.

KEY FUNCTION: build_debt_schedule(assumptions, max_iterations, tolerance)
   Returns: (debt_df, is_df) — both fully reconciled DataFrames

DEBT SCHEDULE LINE ITEMS:
   Opening Debt Balance        (what we owe at start of year)
   Interest Expense            (opening balance × interest rate)
   FCF Available               (from income statement)
   Debt Repayment              (min(FCF, opening balance) — can't repay more than owed)
   Closing Debt Balance        (opening − repayment)
   Leverage Ratio              (opening debt / EBITDA — key bank covenant metric)

CASH SWEEP LOGIC:
   When cash_sweep = True (default), ALL available FCF goes to debt repayment.
   This maximizes debt paydown speed, which maximizes equity return at exit.
   Alternative: scheduled amortization (fixed repayment each year regardless of FCF).
   In real deals, both exist — senior debt has mandatory amortization,
   revolving credit can be swept with excess cash.

LEVERAGE RATIO:
   Debt/EBITDA ratio is how banks measure a company's debt burden.
   At acquisition: might be 5-6x (highly leveraged)
   By exit year: should fall to 2-3x (demonstrates successful deleveraging)
   Banks typically require covenants: "Debt/EBITDA must not exceed 6x"

FILE 4: returns.py
────────────────────────────────────────

PURPOSE:
   Calculates the PE firm's investment returns and decomposes them
   into the three value creation drivers. This is the payoff module
   where all prior calculations culminate in the final investment verdict.

KEY FUNCTION: calculate_returns(assumptions)
   1. Calls build_debt_schedule() to get reconciled IS and debt schedule
   2. Reads exit year EBITDA from IS
   3. Computes Exit EV = Exit EBITDA × Exit Multiple
   4. Computes Exit Equity = Exit EV − Remaining Debt at Exit
   5. Builds cash flow stream for IRR calculation
   6. Computes IRR using numpy_financial.irr()
   7. Computes MoM = Exit Equity / Equity Invested
   8. Runs return attribution decomposition

IRR EXPLAINED:
   IRR = Internal Rate of Return = the discount rate that makes the
   Net Present Value (NPV) of all cash flows equal to zero.

   In simple terms: "What annual interest rate would a savings account
   need to offer to give you the same result as this investment?"

   Cash flow stream passed to IRR function:
     Year 0  : -₹336 Cr    (equity invested — negative because cash OUT)
     Year 1  :  ₹0          (no dividends during hold — PE standard)
     Year 2  :  ₹0
     Year 3  :  ₹0
     Year 4  :  ₹0
     Year 5  : +₹X Cr      (exit equity proceeds — positive because cash IN)

   numpy_financial.irr() solves: 0 = -336 + X/(1+r)^5 for r.

   PE BENCHMARKS:
     IRR > 25% : Exceptional — top quartile deal
     IRR 20-25%: Good — meets standard PE hurdle rate
     IRR 15-20%: Marginal — below hurdle, likely rejected
     IRR < 15% : Weak — does not justify illiquidity premium over public markets

MoM (MONEY-ON-MONEY) EXPLAINED:
   MoM = Exit Equity / Equity Invested
   Simple ratio. If you invested ₹336 Cr and got back ₹910 Cr: MoM = 2.7x

   WHY MoM AND IRR TOGETHER?
   MoM ignores time. A 3x MoM in 3 years is phenomenal (44% IRR).
   A 3x MoM in 10 years is mediocre (11.6% IRR).
   IRR accounts for time. MoM shows raw magnitude of return.
   PE reports BOTH because they capture different dimensions of performance.

RETURN ATTRIBUTION:
   Decomposes total equity gain into three buckets:

   a) EBITDA Growth Contribution:
      How much equity was created purely from the company becoming more profitable?
      Calculated as: (Exit EBITDA × Entry Multiple) − Entry Equity
      This isolates the "company grew" effect by holding multiple constant.

   b) Multiple Expansion:
      How much equity was created from selling at a higher multiple than entry?
      Calculated as: Exit EBITDA × (Exit Multiple − Entry Multiple)
      This isolates the "market re-rated the company" effect.

   c) Debt Paydown:
      How much equity was created purely from debt being repaid?
      Calculated as: Total Debt − Remaining Debt at Exit
      Every rupee of debt repaid transfers directly to equity holders at exit.

   EXAMPLE ATTRIBUTION (base case):
     Equity Invested          : ₹336 Cr
     EBITDA Growth            : +₹287 Cr  (37% of value created)
     Multiple Expansion       : +₹192 Cr  (25% of value created)
     Debt Paydown             : +₹373 Cr  (48% of value created)  ← largest driver
     Exit Equity              : ₹908 Cr
     MoM                      : 2.7x

FILE 5: scenarios.py
────────────────────────────────────────

PURPOSE:
   Runs the full LBO model under four different economic environments.
   Tests the investment thesis under both favourable and adverse conditions.
   The output tells you if the deal has "margin of safety."

WHY SCENARIOS MATTER:
   Every assumption in the model is uncertain. Revenue might grow slower.
   Competition might compress margins. The exit market might weaken.
   Scenarios answer: "How badly do things have to go before we lose money?"

FOUR SCENARIOS DEFINED:

   BULL CASE:
     Revenue CAGR      : 18%     (strong consumer demand, new store rollout)
     EBITDA Margin     : 22%     (operating leverage, cost control)
     Exit Multiple     : 10x     (market is buoyant, premium for quality)
     Interest Rate     : 9%      (negotiated better debt terms)
     Narrative         : Everything goes right. Management over-delivers.

   BASE CASE:
     Revenue CAGR      : 12%     (steady historical growth rate)
     EBITDA Margin     : 18%     (margins hold stable)
     Exit Multiple     : 9x      (exit at same market conditions as entry)
     Interest Rate     : 10%     (standard market rate)
     Narrative         : Management case. What was presented to investment committee.

   BEAR CASE:
     Revenue CAGR      : 6%      (economic slowdown, competition intensifies)
     EBITDA Margin     : 14%     (food cost inflation, wage increases)
     Exit Multiple     : 7x      (market de-rates QSR sector)
     Interest Rate     : 11%     (credit tightening, lender demands premium)
     Narrative         : Things go wrong. But company survives. This is the test.

   DISTRESSED CASE:
     Revenue CAGR      : 2%      (near-recessionary — barely growing)
     EBITDA Margin     : 11%     (severe margin erosion)
     Exit Multiple     : 5.5x    (fire-sale conditions)
     Interest Rate     : 12%     (distressed debt premium)
     Narrative         : Worst realistic scenario. Tests if equity is wiped out.

SCENARIO DESIGN PRINCIPLE:
   Notice that all four variables move together in the Bear/Distressed cases.
   This is intentional. In bad economic times, growth slows AND margins compress
   AND exit markets weaken AND borrowing costs rise — SIMULTANEOUSLY.
   Modelling them independently would understate the true downside risk.
   Correlated scenario design is what distinguishes professional risk analysis.

KEY QUESTION THE BEAR CASE ANSWERS:
   "If everything goes wrong, do we at least get our money back?"
   If Bear Case MoM > 1.0x → equity is not wiped out → deal has downside protection.
   If Bear Case MoM < 1.0x → equity is destroyed in bad times → deal is too risky.

KEY FUNCTIONS:
   run_all_scenarios(base_assumptions)    → runs all 4 scenarios, returns results dict
   build_scenario_table(all_results)      → builds comparison DataFrame for printing
   plot_scenario_comparison(all_results)  → generates 2-panel bar chart

FILE 6: sensitivity.py
────────────────────────────────────────

PURPOSE:
   The most visually impressive component of the project. Generates
   color-coded heatmaps showing how IRR changes across a grid of
   two simultaneous assumption changes. Used by PE firms to set
   maximum bid prices and minimum acceptable exit requirements.

WHAT IS SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS?
   Instead of asking "what is the IRR?" (one number), we ask:
   "How does IRR change as Entry Multiple varies from 6x to 11x
    AND Exit Multiple varies from 7x to 11x simultaneously?"

   The result is a 6×5 matrix — 30 different IRR calculations —
   displayed as a color-coded table where green = good return
   and red = value destruction.

TWO HEATMAPS GENERATED:

   HEATMAP 1 — Deal Pricing Sensitivity:
     Rows    : Entry EV/EBITDA Multiple [6x, 7x, 8x, 9x, 10x, 11x]
     Columns : Exit EV/EBITDA Multiple  [7x, 8x, 9x, 10x, 11x]
     Values  : IRR (%)
     Purpose : Tells you "how much can we pay for this company?" and
               "what exit do we need to clear our return hurdle?"
     Use     : Investment committee uses this to set maximum bid price.
               "We cannot pay more than 8.5x entry under any scenario."

   HEATMAP 2 — Operational Sensitivity:
     Rows    : Revenue CAGR    [4%, 6%, 8%, 10%, 12%, 15%, 18%]
     Columns : EBITDA Margin   [12%, 14%, 16%, 18%, 20%, 22%]
     Values  : IRR (%)
     Purpose : Tells you "how dependent is our return on management execution?"
               Shows whether the deal works even with below-plan operations.
     Use     : Operations team uses this to set minimum performance targets
               for portfolio company management post-acquisition.

COLOR CONVENTION (consistent with industry standard):
   Dark Green  (#1A5C38) : IRR > 25%  — Exceptional, top-quartile return
   Green       (#27AE60) : IRR 20-25% — Good, clears PE hurdle rate
   Yellow      (#D4AC0D) : IRR 15-20% — Marginal, below hurdle
   Orange      (#CA6F1E) : IRR 10-15% — Weak, consider passing
   Red         (#922B21) : IRR < 10%  — Avoid, value destruction territory

BASE CASE CELL:
   The base case combination is highlighted with a white border on the heatmap,
   so you can instantly see where the modelled scenario sits in the grid.

SECTION 7: KEY FINANCIAL CONCEPTS GLOSSARY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortization):
   The most widely used measure of a company's operating cash generation.
   Strips out financing costs (interest), tax effects, and non-cash charges
   (D&A) to show pure operational profitability. LBO valuations are almost
   always expressed as multiples of EBITDA.

ENTERPRISE VALUE (EV):
   The total value of a company — what it would cost to buy the entire business.
   EV = Market Cap + Net Debt (for public companies)
   EV = Purchase Price (in an M&A transaction)
   Important: EV is the value of the BUSINESS. Equity value is what's left
   for shareholders after repaying debt.

EV/EBITDA MULTIPLE:
   The valuation multiple. "We're buying at 8x EBITDA" means:
   Purchase Price = 8 × EBITDA.
   Industry and growth determine what multiple is "fair."
   Higher multiple = more expensive. Lower multiple = cheaper.

FREE CASH FLOW (FCF):
   The actual cash a business generates that can be used for debt repayment,
   dividends, or reinvestment. NOT the same as profit.
   FCF = EBITDA − Tax − Capex − Change in Working Capital
   In LBOs, FCF is the debt repayment engine.

IRR (Internal Rate of Return):
   The annualized return on an investment. Accounts for both magnitude
   and timing of cash flows. Solved by finding r where NPV = 0.
   Industry benchmark for PE: 20-25%+.

MoM (Money-on-Money Multiple):
   Total cash returned / Total cash invested.
   Does not account for time. Used alongside IRR.
   PE benchmark: 2.5x-3x over 5 years.

LEVERAGE RATIO (Debt/EBITDA):
   How many years of EBITDA it would take to repay all debt.
   Banks use this as a covenant. LBOs typically enter at 5-6x,
   and must reduce it over the hold period.

CASH SWEEP:
   Using all available Free Cash Flow to repay debt as fast as possible.
   Maximizes debt paydown speed → maximizes equity value at exit.

HURDLE RATE:
   The minimum acceptable IRR for a PE fund. Typically 20%.
   If a deal can't generate 20%+ IRR, the fund won't invest.
   Why 20%? Because PE investors lock up money for 5-7 years.
   They demand a premium over public market returns (~12-15%).

MULTIPLE EXPANSION:
   When exit EV/EBITDA multiple > entry EV/EBITDA multiple.
   Creates instant value even if operations stay flat.
   The inverse (multiple compression) destroys value.

CAPEX (Capital Expenditure):
   Cash spent on physical assets — stores, equipment, property.
   Reduces FCF. High-capex businesses are harder LBO candidates.
   Asset-light businesses (software, food chains, logistics) are preferred.

WORKING CAPITAL:
   Money tied up in the operating cycle: inventory + receivables − payables.
   Growth consumes working capital (you need more cash to fund bigger operations).
   This is why fast-growing companies sometimes run out of cash despite being profitable.

RETURN ATTRIBUTION:
   Decomposing total investment return into its three sources:
   EBITDA growth + Multiple expansion + Debt paydown.
   Tells you which lever created the most value in a deal.

SECTION 8: LIBRARIES USED AND WHY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

numpy (np):
   Core numerical computing. Used for array operations, NaN handling,
   matrix construction in sensitivity analysis. Foundation of all
   scientific Python computing.

numpy_financial (npf):
   Financial functions separated from numpy core in v1.0.
   We use npf.irr() — the IRR calculator. Takes a list of cash flows,
   returns the discount rate where NPV = 0. Essential for returns.py.

pandas (pd):
   Data manipulation and tabular output. Every financial table in the
   project (income statement, debt schedule, scenario table) is a
   pandas DataFrame. Makes row/column operations and printing clean.

matplotlib (plt):
   Core plotting library. Used for all bar charts, waterfall charts,
   debt paydown visualizations, and as the base for heatmaps.
   All charts use a dark theme (#0f0f0f background) for visual impact.

seaborn (sns):
   Statistical visualization built on matplotlib. Used specifically
   for sns.heatmap() in sensitivity.py — handles the color-coded
   matrix visualization with annotations efficiently.

openpyxl:
   Excel file creation and formatting in Python. Used in main.py to
   build the 5-sheet tearsheet with custom colors, fonts, borders,
   and merged cells. Produces a .xlsx file that looks professionally
   formatted without any manual work.

copy (standard library):
   Used in scenarios.py and sensitivity.py to deep-copy the base
   DealAssumptions object before applying overrides. Ensures the
   base case is never mutated by scenario/sensitivity runs.

dataclasses (standard library):
   Used in assumptions.py. @dataclass automatically generates
   __init__, __repr__ etc. from class fields. Clean, readable way
   to define a configuration object with type annotations.

argparse (standard library):
   Command-line argument parsing in main.py. Allows overriding
   assumptions from the terminal without editing source code.
   Standard Python approach to building CLI tools.

SECTION 10: HOW TO CUSTOMIZE FOR ANOTHER COMPANY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

To model a DIFFERENT company (e.g., Sapphire Foods, Metro Brands):

1. Download the company's last 3 Annual Reports from BSE/NSE
2. Extract these numbers:
   — Revenue (last year)
   — EBITDA (or calculate as Revenue − COGS − Employee costs − Other opex)
   — Depreciation & Amortization
   — Capex
   — Current Assets and Current Liabilities (for working capital)
   — Existing Debt

3. Open assumptions.py and update:
   company_name          = "Your Company Name"
   entry_year_ebitda     = [actual EBITDA in Cr]
   revenue_base          = [actual Revenue in Cr]
   revenue_cagr          = [realistic growth rate based on history]
   ebitda_margin         = [EBITDA / Revenue]
   depreciation_pct_revenue = [D&A / Revenue]
   capex_pct_revenue     = [Capex / Revenue]
   entry_ev_multiple     = [research comparable M&A transactions]

4. Run python main.py — everything else updates automatically.

HOW TO FIND COMPARABLE TRANSACTION MULTIPLES:
   Search: "[Industry] M&A transactions EV/EBITDA India"
   Sources: Refinitiv, Bloomberg (if you have access), news articles
   Rule of thumb: QSR/FMCG trades at 8-12x EBITDA in India currently

   Real LBOs use layered debt structures:
   — Senior Secured Debt (cheapest, paid first)
   — Mezzanine Debt (higher rate, subordinate)

   Model each tranche separately in debt_schedule.py.

EXTENSION 2: MONTE CARLO SIMULATION
   Instead of 4 fixed scenarios, run 10,000 random simulations
   where assumptions are drawn from probability distributions.
   Output: IRR distribution histogram showing probability of
   clearing the hurdle rate. Much more sophisticated risk analysis.

EXTENSION 3: LIVE DATA INTEGRATION
   Use the yfinance library to pull real stock/financial data.
   Auto-populate assumptions.py from actual BSE/NSE filings.
   Makes the model dynamic rather than static.

EXTENSION 4: STREAMLIT DASHBOARD
   Convert the model into an interactive web app using Streamlit.
   Users slide bars to change assumptions, charts update in real-time.
   Much more impressive for a portfolio demo than static charts.

EXTENSION 5: DCF VALUATION MODULE
   Add a separate DCF (Discounted Cash Flow) valuation to cross-check
   the LBO entry price. Compare DCF intrinsic value vs LBO purchase price.
   Full buy-side analysis in one tool.

When a PE interviewer asks about this project, here's how to answer:

Q: "Walk me through this LBO model."
A: "I built a full LBO model in Python for Devyani International —
    the KFC and Pizza Hut franchisee in India. The model assumes an
    8x entry multiple on ₹120 Cr EBITDA — that's a ₹960 Cr acquisition
    funded 65% with debt and 35% equity. Over 5 years, the company's
    free cash flow pays down the debt, and I exit at 9x on a higher
    EBITDA base. Base case gives 23% IRR and 2.7x MoM. The biggest
    return driver is debt paydown at roughly 48% of value created."

Q: "How did you handle the circular reference between interest and FCF?"
A: "The income statement needs interest expense to compute FCF, but
    interest expense depends on the debt balance, which depends on
    prior FCF — a classic LBO circular dependency. I solved it
    iteratively: start with a simplified flat-debt assumption, compute
    FCF, build a debt schedule, feed the real opening balances back
    into the income statement, and repeat until closing balances
    converge within ₹0.01 Cr. Typically takes 3-5 iterations."

Q: "What does your Bear Case tell you?"
A: "In the Bear Case — 6% revenue growth, 14% EBITDA margin, 7x exit,
    11% interest rate — the model generates around 11% IRR and 1.7x MoM.
    That means even in an adverse scenario, equity is not destroyed.
    The deal has margin of safety. The Distressed Case at near-recession
    assumptions still returns capital at about 1.1x MoM — barely, but
    the company doesn't default because the cash sweep still services
    the debt even with compressed margins."

Q: "What would change your return attribution most?"
A: "Debt paydown is currently the biggest driver at 48%. If we extended
    the hold period from 5 to 7 years, debt paydown would increase but
    IRR would likely fall due to time dilution. The most leveraged return
    driver per unit of management effort is multiple expansion — one
    extra turn on exit EBITDA of ₹215 Cr adds ₹215 Cr of enterprise
    value directly to equity. That's why PE firms obsess over exit timing."
